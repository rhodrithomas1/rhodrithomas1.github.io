How DeepSkyScout Works

What the controls do, what the charts show, and how the SNR ranking is estimated.

Quick start
  1. Pick your location (or add your own).
  2. Select telescope + reducer + camera so the field-of-view and image scale are correct.
  3. Set the horizon (simple altitude, or load a custom horizon profile).
  4. Use Tonight’s Visibility to see the sky track, then sort the table by SNR (Advanced mode).
  5. Switch to Sky Image (Aladin) to check framing and the surrounding field.

Tip: you can share a setup using the URL parameters (location/equipment/date/horizon). Opening the link restores the same planner state.

Planner inputs

Location + date
  • Location sets latitude/longitude for altitude/azimuth tracks.
  • Date chooses the night window (Sunset → Sunrise for that date at your location).
  • The “Tonight’s Visibility” chart uses that window to compute when each object is above your chosen horizon.
Horizon
  • Simple horizon: use a fixed altitude like 20°.
  • Custom horizon: load a .hrz profile so visibility respects local obstructions (trees/buildings/hills).
  • The planner only counts an object as “visible” when its altitude is above the horizon profile at that azimuth.
Equipment
  • Telescope + reducer sets focal length and aperture (light-gathering area).
  • Camera sets sensor size and pixel size, which determines field-of-view and image scale.
  • Field-of-view is used for framing (min-size filter) and for the SNR measurement patch size (via arcsec/pixel).
Sky quality + filters
  • Bortle / SQM estimates the sky brightness background.
  • Broadband RGB uses the selected magnitude band from your JSON: B, V, or R.
  • Narrowband (nebulae) uses an Hα bandpass selection: 20 nm / 9 nm / 6 nm / 3 nm.

Broadband is used automatically for galaxies/clusters/most non-nebula targets; narrowband is used for nebula targets.

Charts and views

Tonight’s Visibility
  • Shows the selected object’s track in Alt/Az over the night (polar plot).
  • Optional grid overlay: Alt-Az or Equatorial.
  • Moon path is drawn on the same plot, with a phase icon in the corner.
  • Visibility duration is computed by stepping through the night and checking altitude vs horizon.
Sky Image (Aladin)
  • Interactive sky viewer (pan/zoom) centered on the selected target.
  • Switch surveys (e.g. DSS2, IR, Hα depending on what’s available).
  • Use this to sanity-check framing and nearby bright stars/nebula structure.
Monthly Visibility
  • Shows how visibility changes across the year by month for your location/horizon.
  • This is a planning view: it helps you pick “seasonal” targets.
Moon info
  • Moon phase is shown as illumination (%) and as a small phase graphic.
  • The planner also tracks the closest separation between Moon and target during the night.
  • That separation feeds into the SNR moonlight penalty described below.

The object table

Sorting, filtering, advanced columns
  • The table is designed for ranking and quick comparisons.
  • Advanced mode enables SNR sorting and shows extra columns like Mag and Size.
  • Use the minimum size slider to hide tiny targets that will be under-sampled or look “star-like” for your setup.

SNR ranking model

What SNR means here

DeepSkyScout’s SNR is a relative planning score used to rank targets for your chosen setup. It combines target brightness, angular size (a surface-brightness proxy), sky background (Bortle/SQM), filter bandwidth, altitude/airmass, and moonlight. It is designed to be consistent and useful for comparing targets, rather than being a fully calibrated exposure-time calculator.

Core equation

For a small measurement patch on the sensor, the score follows the standard background-limited form:

\[ \mathrm{SNR}=\frac{S}{\sqrt{S+B_{\mathrm{sky}}+B_{\mathrm{moon}}}} \]

Where \(S\) is the target signal (photons in the patch) and the \(B\) terms are background photons accumulated in the same patch. The final displayed SNR is capped (currently at 10000) to keep the UI stable.

Step 1 — Convert catalog brightness to photons

Broadband (RGB) uses the selected band magnitude (Blue→B, Green→V, Red→R). The code converts magnitude to an approximate photon flux using:

\[ F_{\gamma} = F_{\gamma,0}\;10^{-0.4\,m} \]

\(F_{\gamma,0}\) is an internal zero-point constant (and the code uses a representative central wavelength and bandpass per band). This keeps ranking consistent even if absolute calibration is approximate.

Narrowband (Hα) uses integrated Hα line flux (when available in the JSON) and converts energy flux to photon flux:

\[ F_{\gamma,\mathrm{H\alpha}} = F_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}\;\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}}{h\,c} \]

with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}\approx 656.3\ \mathrm{nm}\). Narrowband sky background is reduced roughly in proportion to the selected filter width (20/9/6/3 nm).

Step 2 — Telescope throughput and collecting area

Flux is turned into detected photons using the telescope collecting area and an overall efficiency factor:

\[ A_{\mathrm{tel}} = \pi\left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2,\qquad S \propto F_{\gamma}\;A_{\mathrm{tel}}\;\eta\;t \]

\(D\) is aperture, \(\eta\) bundles throughput/QE-like efficiency, and \(t\) is the integration time used internally for scoring. (Because SNR is used as a ranking, the exact choice of \(t\) mostly scales all scores similarly.)

Step 3 — Size → surface brightness proxy

DeepSkyScout spreads integrated flux over the object’s angular area to estimate a surface brightness proxy. Using the catalog ellipse (major/minor axes in arcminutes):

\[ A_{\mathrm{obj}} \approx \pi\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)\,(60^2)\ \mathrm{arcsec}^2 \]

To avoid “infinite SNR” from summing over an entire huge object, the score uses a fixed measurement patch (a small \(N\times N\) pixel box, typically 5×5) based on your image scale:

\[ A_{\mathrm{patch}} \approx \left(N\cdot s\right)^2,\qquad s=\mathrm{arcsec/pixel} \]

The fraction of total object flux assumed to fall into the patch is:

\[ f=\min\left(1,\frac{A_{\mathrm{patch}}}{A_{\mathrm{obj}}}\right),\qquad S \leftarrow S\cdot f \]

This is why compact objects (planetary nebulae, compact galaxies) can score higher than very extended diffuse objects.

Step 4 — Sky background (SQM/Bortle)

Sky brightness is treated as a background surface brightness (mag/arcsec²) that is converted into photons per arcsec², then scaled to the patch area and bandpass:

\[ B_{\mathrm{sky}} \propto F_{\gamma,\mathrm{sky}} \;A_{\mathrm{tel}}\;\eta\;t\;A_{\mathrm{patch}} \]

Narrowband background is reduced relative to broadband by the chosen Hα filter width. Broadband uses the selected RGB bandpass.

The model is primarily “signal vs sky background”; detailed camera noise terms (read noise/dark current) are not the focus.

Altitude, horizon, and meridian

For the night window (Sunset→Sunrise), DeepSkyScout samples the object’s altitude/azimuth and only treats it as usable when it is above your selected horizon profile. The meridian time is the time of maximum altitude.

Higher altitude (lower airmass) improves results in practice; the score applies a mild altitude/airmass weighting so targets low in the murk are penalized compared to those near culmination.

Moon impact on SNR

How moonlight is applied
  • The Moon position is sampled through the night (only when the target is above your horizon).
  • Extra sky brightness depends on:
    • Moon phase (illumination fraction)
    • Moon altitude (higher Moon → brighter background)
    • Angular separation between Moon and target (closer → worse)
  • That extra brightness is converted into additional sky background photons and added to the SNR denominator.
  • Broadband is penalized more strongly than narrowband.

The moonlight model is a heuristic scattering/brightening approximation meant for planning. It is not a site-specific atmospheric model.

Assumptions & limitations

Important caveats
  • Catalog data quality: magnitudes, sizes, and line fluxes vary in completeness and quality.
  • Integrated magnitude ≠ surface brightness: extended objects with bright cores and faint halos are simplified into one ellipse.
  • Camera noise terms (read noise, dark current, gain, saturation) are not modeled in detail; the score is mainly signal vs sky background.
  • Throughput/QE are treated as simple efficiency factors (not full spectral curves).
  • Weather/seeing/transparency aren’t included; they can dominate night-to-night results.
